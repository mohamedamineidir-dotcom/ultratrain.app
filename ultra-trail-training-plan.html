Ultra Trail Training Plan: The Complete 4-Phase Guide

A successful ultra trail training plan spans 12 to 24 weeks and is built backward from race day in four phases: Base, Build, Peak, and Taper. Here's how each phase works, how much to run and climb each week, and the rules that keep you from getting hurt before you get to the start line.

How long should your plan be?

Plan length depends mostly on race distance and your current fitness:

Base phase

This is where most ultra trail runners should spend the most time and the biggest mistake people make is skipping it. The goal is aerobic volume and elevation tolerance, not speed. Weekly long runs grow steadily, vertical gain (D+) sessions are introduced early so your legs adapt to descending-related eccentric load before race-specific intensity shows up.

Build phase

Race-specific work starts here: climbing repeats, tempo efforts on tired legs, and back-to-back long runs (a long run Saturday followed by another long run Sunday) that simulate cumulative race fatigue better than any single long run can. This is also where intermediate B-races, if you have any, tend to fall.

Peak phase

Short and sharp. Sessions mimic race terrain and effort, but total volume is already easing off the Build-phase high point so you're sharpening fitness rather than still trying to build it.

Taper phase

Volume drops progressively over the final 2-3 weeks while intensity is mostly preserved, so your legs feel fresh without losing race sharpness.

The two rules that keep an ultra trail plan safe

  1. The 10% rule: never increase weekly distance or elevation gain by more than about 10% from one week to the next. This is the single biggest lever against overuse injury in ultra training.
  2. Recovery weeks every 3rd or 4th week: cut volume to roughly 80-85% of the prior load week (about a 15% reduction) on a 2:1 or 3:1 build-to-recovery cycle. Skipping these is how runners arrive at Peak phase already fatigued.

Weekly session structure

A well-built week mixes these session types rather than just piling up easy kilometers:

  • Long run: the cornerstone session, growing progressively through Base and Build
  • Back-to-back long runs: introduced in Build phase to train on accumulated fatigue
  • Vertical gain / D+ sessions: dedicated climbing and descending work matched to your race's elevation profile
  • Intervals & tempo: race-specific intensity, typically 1-2 sessions per week
  • Recovery runs: easy effort between harder sessions to aid adaptation

Nutrition is part of the plan, not an afterthought

Race-day fueling needs to be trained, not improvised. As a starting point: 4-6 calories per kg of body weight per hour, 400-800 ml/hour of fluid depending on heat, and roughly 500-700 mg/hour of sodium for ultra-distance efforts. Any long run over 2 hours is a good opportunity for gut training: practicing your exact race-day nutrition so your digestive system adapts before race day, not during it.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an ultra trail training plan be?

Most ultra trail training plans run 12 to 24 weeks. A 50K with a solid aerobic base can be built in 12-14 weeks; a 100-mile race with major elevation gain benefits from 20-24 weeks so the Base and Build phases aren't rushed.

What are the phases of an ultra trail training plan?

Four phases, built backward from race day: Base (aerobic foundation and vertical gain tolerance), Build (race-specific climbing and back-to-back long runs), Peak (sharpening at race-like effort), and Taper (volume reduction so you arrive fresh).

How much should I increase my weekly distance or elevation?

No more than about 10% week-over-week for both distance and elevation gain (D+). Increasing faster than that is one of the most common causes of overuse injury in ultra training.

How often do I need a recovery week?

Every 3rd or 4th week, cut volume to roughly 80-85% of your prior load week (about a 15% reduction). This 3:1 or 2:1 build-to-recovery cycle is what most structured ultra plans, including UltraTrain's, are built around.

Do I need to taper before an intermediate (B or C) race?

Yes. A 3-5 day mini-taper before a B-race and 3-7 days of recovery after it keeps the intermediate race useful as a fitness benchmark without derailing your main training block.
